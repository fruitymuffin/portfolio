Reflections

Apply principles of sustainability to create viable systems

One of my duties at Labyrinth Escape Rooms was to maintain the electronic systems of all the active escape rooms. Casual workers would identify problems during use, and the problem would be listed on a whiteboard, or a WhatsApp message was sent. Initially, I thought this was quite informal, but no issues occurred. Over time, however, issues requiring fixes would become forgotten. The whiteboard would be cleared accidentally, and messages in the chat could go unnoticed. This led to an increase in maintenance issues being unresolved, eventually causing issues for customers.

I met with my supervisor to discuss how we could improve things. He recommended getting a book to keep track of issues. After the meeting, I started to think that a paper system was not a sustainable, modern system to be implemented in 2022. Especially in a business that dealt with so much paper already. I was not very comfortable adding more paper to the office when there were already considerable amounts used for consent forms, and logging purposes. I recommend using a web-based ticketing system to keep track of issues. I was worried that my supervisor would be apprehensive since it would require a time investment to create the system, but he was happy to pursue it. However, he said that it would need to wait at least a month.

At the time, I doubted why no-one considered this before me. Especially since it had caused problems before. I was frustrated at the delay and was unsure why we could not implement the new system immediately. I now see that it is difficult to delay urgent maintenance to create new systems. I appreciate that the long-term fix for a problem is hard to tackle when you are swamped by short-term issues. Engineers Australia (2017) explains how efficient management systems can increase the sustainability of a business by increasing efficiency and decreasing waste. I understand that to accomplish this, a sacrifice may be necessary. In this case, planned downtime was scheduled to ensure that the new system could be created and urgent maintenance matters solved.

I am grateful my supervisor saw fit to spend time and money to fix the root of a problem. The new system now allows many issues to be tracked simultaneously and for updates and info on each issue to be stored. I learned that it is difficult to implement sustainable work systems while operating a business. In the future I hope to spend more time considering sustainable approaches before starting new tasks/activities. I believe this can save time/resources later down the track.

Professional Practice within intercultural and global contexts

During my time as a lab demonstrator at UTS teaching a programming class, my role was to roam about the room and assist students with exercises and assignments. A significant portion of the class were international students, and some struggled with English which made it difficult to communicate complicated topics. During some 3-hour classes, I would find myself spending more time with the students who were not very proficient in English that had fallen behind due to language difficulties. As a result, some other students who were having trouble were not getting much support.

I mentioned in passing to the lecturer that more lab staff would be beneficial but ultimately scheduling issues meant this wasn’t immediately feasible. During class I tried to spread my attention as evenly as possible but inevitably someone was struggling and not receiving the ideal help. I felt frustrated about the situation because ideally there would be time to spend time with all students who had questions, but ultimately, I settled for the reality.

On the one hand, I believe it is the lab tutors’ responsibility to provide adequate assistance. Conversely English capabilities are expected in the university and other students should not miss out on assistance because of it. The Australian Curriculum Assessment and Reporting Authority (2024) highlights the importance for empathy when working in intercultural environments, and the presence of unconscious bias. This experience allowed me to recognize the difficult reality for all parties when these situations arise. Sometimes problems cannot be easily solved within the hierarchical framework of your employment organization/industry, but it is important to proceed with empathy for everyone involved.

Throughout our adult life it is reinforced that employers want excellent communication skills, and of course it is common sense that this is necessary for professional success. I believe that given this expectation, universities are responsible for ensuring that English competency is adequately taught.

This experience made me reflect on the fact that the prospect of studying or working in a non-English speaking country is daunting and deserves understanding and respect. I sympathize with both the students struggling through university in a foreign language, and those who must compete for assistance during face-to-face classes. Upon reflection of my own actions, I could have communicated my thoughts on the issue more thoroughly, rather than only mentioning it in passing.

While frustration and blame are normal reactions to difficult circumstances and trouble communicating, I now think the only real solution is for all parties to be responsible for what they can do to remedy the situation. Going forward, I have renewed patience for people facing language difficulties and hope to encourage a positive environment for everyone to work together.
